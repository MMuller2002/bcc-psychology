Key Terms
Reliability
Consistency of a measure — does it give the same result under the same conditions?
Validity
Accuracy — does the measure actually measure what it claims to measure?
Cronbach's Alpha (α)
A statistic measuring internal consistency. Acceptable: α > 0.7. You can be reliable but not valid.
Social Desirability Bias
Answering in ways that look good, rather than truthfully — a major threat to self-report validity.
HRV (Heart Rate Variability)
Variation between heartbeats — a measure of autonomic nervous system balance, NOT heart rate.
GSR / Skin Conductance
Sympathetic arousal measure — detects excitement AND fear (cannot tell them apart).
Inference Problem
Physiological signals indicate biological states — inferring psychological states from them requires caution.

Direct Instruction — Four Categories
10–28 min · Notes · Resource Sheet 13
▼

Complete the overview table on Resource Sheet 13 as your teacher explains each category. The critical insight across all four:

Self-Report: Only the participant has direct access to their subjective experience. No instrument can measure "what it feels like" except asking. But asking introduces bias.
Behavioural Observation: Captures what people actually DO, not what they say they do. Removes self-report bias — but introduces observer effects and requires costly, trained observers.
Physiological Measures: Objective biological recordings — but they measure the body, not the mind. Every physiological inference about a mental state requires careful justification.
Neuroimaging: The most dramatic-looking measure — but suffers from the reverse inference problem: seeing amygdala activation and concluding "they felt fear" is not valid without corroborating evidence. fMRI measures blood flow, not thoughts.

Direct Instruction — Reliability and Validity Framework
10–28 min · Notes · Resource Sheet 14
▼
The scale analogy: A scale that gives different readings each time = unreliable. A scale that consistently reads 5kg too heavy = reliable but NOT valid. You need both. And Cronbach's alpha measures internal consistency — one type of reliability — NOT validity. This is the most common confusion in psychology research.

Direct Instruction — Four Physiological Measures
10–28 min · Notes · Resource Sheet 15
▼
HRV (Heart Rate Variability): Variation between consecutive heartbeats — NOT the same as heart rate. High HRV = healthy parasympathetic balance. Low HRV = sympathetic dominance / stress. Measurable by smartwatch (PPG). Key confound: age, fitness, caffeine, body position.
GSR / Skin Conductance: Measures sympathetic arousal via sweat glands. Fast and sensitive. Major limitation: measures arousal, not valence — excitement and fear produce identical signals. Cannot tell them apart.
Cortisol Assay: Salivary cortisol peaks 15–20 min after a stressor. Hair cortisol = 1 month per centimetre (retrospective chronic stress measure). Critical: control for the cortisol awakening response (CAR) — cortisol peaks naturally within 30 min of waking regardless of stress.
EEG Extension — ERPs: Event-Related Potentials are brain responses to specific stimuli. The P300 wave (300ms after a surprising stimulus) indicates attentional processing. Alpha asymmetry (right vs left frontal) correlates with approach vs withdrawal motivation in emotion research.
📉
