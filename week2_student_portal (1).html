Key Terms
Circadian Rhythm
An internal ~24-hour biological cycle regulating sleep, hormones, body temperature, and alertness.
SCN
Suprachiasmatic Nucleus — the brain's master clock, located in the hypothalamus.
Zeitgeber
"Time-giver" — external cues (light, meals, exercise) that synchronise the biological clock.
Adenosine
A chemical that builds up while you're awake, creating sleep pressure (Process S).
Melatonin
A "darkness signal" hormone — it opens the gate for sleep but does not cause sleep directly.
Social Jetlag
The mismatch between your biological clock and your social schedule — worst on weekends.
Phase Delay
The adolescent shift of the circadian clock ~1–3 hours later than adults — biological, not behavioural.

Lesson 1 · AC9S10H03 · Explain

Circadian Rhythms —
Your Internal 24-Hour Clock

By the end of this lesson you'll be able to explain how your biological clock is set, what a zeitgeber is, and why adolescents naturally run 1–3 hours later than adults — using precise biological language.

⏱ 70 min SCN · Melatonin · Zeitgeber 👥 Pairs + debate
🦉
Hook — The Chronotype Poll
0–10 min · Whole class
▼

Are you a lark, an owl, or something in between? Select your natural sleep tendency — your teacher will tally the class.

🐦 MORNING LARK Naturally awake early; tired by 9–10pm
🦉 NIGHT OWL Alert late; struggles to rise before 8am
🐦 HUMMINGBIRD Flexible — can adapt to either extreme
⚠️ The Science of Chronotypes

Chronotypes are heritable — partly determined by genes (e.g. the PER3 gene). Adolescents experience a biological phase delay that pushes their entire clock 1–3 hours later. Calling a night-owl teenager "lazy" is biologically incorrect.

🧠
Direct Instruction — SCN, Zeitgebers & the 24-Hour Cycle
10–27 min · Notes
▼

Your teacher will explain three layers of circadian biology. Take notes as each section is covered:

🕹 The Master Clock — SCN

The Suprachiasmatic Nucleus sits in your hypothalamus — a cluster of ~20,000 neurons generating an intrinsic cycle of ~24.2 hours. It must be re-set daily by external cues. It coordinates hormones, digestion, immune function, body temperature, and alertness across every organ.

⏰ Zeitgebers — Time Givers

Primary: Light — detected by melanopsin-containing ipRGC cells in your retina. Blue (480nm) wavelength is most potent. Pathway: eyes → SCN → pineal gland → melatonin suppressed.
Secondary: Meal timing, exercise, social schedules, temperature — can shift your clock even without light.

📈 The 24-Hour Alertness Cycle

Key anchor points: cortisol peaks ~6am (CAR) → alertness peaks ~10am → circadian trough ~2–3pm → second peak ~7pm ('forbidden zone') → melatonin rises ~9pm. Adolescent shift: push all of this 1–3 hours later.

Lesson 2 · AC9S10H03 · Explain

Sleep Regulation —
The Chemistry of Falling Asleep

By the end of this lesson you'll be able to explain the two-process model of sleep — including adenosine (Process S) and melatonin (Process C) — and trace exactly what caffeine and naps do to each process.

⏱ 70 min Adenosine · Two-Process Model 🖊 Graph + Scenarios
🪙
Hook — The Adenosine Bank Account
0–10 min · Demonstration
▼

Watch the token demonstration carefully. Each token = 1 hour of wakefulness = adenosine accumulating in your brain.

🪙 The Demo Explained

Tokens represent adenosine — it builds up every waking hour as a byproduct of brain cell activity

The coffee mug = caffeine — it sweeps the tokens away, but they don't disappear. They're still there; you just can't feel them anymore.

Only sleep actually clears adenosine — this is the only true 'reset'

📐
Direct Instruction — Borbély's Two-Process Model
10–28 min · Notes
▼

Sleep is regulated by two independent systems that interact. Both must align for optimal sleep onset.

Process S
Sleep Homeostasis
  • Driven by adenosine build-up
  • Rises continuously while awake
  • Cleared by sleep (especially NREM 3)
  • Caffeine masks but doesn't clear it
  • Naps partially discharge it
Process C
Circadian Drive
  • Driven by SCN (from Lesson 1)
  • SCN promotes wakefulness during day
  • SCN withdraws at night → sleep gate opens
  • Melatonin signals darkness (not sleep itself)
  • Adenosine + melatonin overlap = sleep onset
🧹 Glymphatic Clearance — Advanced

During deep sleep (NREM Stage 3), brain cells shrink ~60%, allowing cerebrospinal fluid to flush metabolic waste — including adenosine and amyloid-beta, the protein implicated in Alzheimer's. Sleep is the brain's cleaning system.

Application — The Melatonin Supplement Debate
47–59 min · Discussion
▼

Melatonin supplements are sold at pharmacies without a prescription. The dose and evidence matters:

Evidence For
  • 0.5–1mg taken 1–2 hrs before target sleep can shift circadian phase
  • Effective for delayed sleep phase disorder (DSPD)
  • Low side-effects at correct physiological doses
Cautions
  • Commercial tablets are typically 5–10mg (10–20× too much)
  • Supplement label doses can be wrong by ±470%
  • Treats the symptom, not the cause (light exposure, schedule)
  • Possible hormonal effects in developing adolescents (under study)

Lesson 3 · AC9S10H04 · Analyse

Lifestyle & Sleep —
What We Do and What It Costs

By the end of this lesson you'll be able to analyse the biological mechanisms behind common sleep disruptors, and examine how societal values — hustle culture, the sleep economy — shape both behaviour and research priorities.

⏱ 70 min AC9S10H04 · Analyse ✍️ TEEL Paragraph
😴
Hook — Your Sleep Debt Audit
0–10 min · Individual → class
▼

Using Resource Sheet 6, fill in your 7-day sleep log and calculate your weekly sleep debt. Then your teacher will collate the class data.

💡 Sleep Debt Facts
  • Adolescents need 8–10 hours per night (National Sleep Foundation)
  • Sleep debt is cumulative — you can't fully repay it with one long sleep
  • A single weekend "sleep-in" shifts your circadian clock later, making Monday morning harder — this is social jetlag
  • Even mild sleep debt (1–2 hours per night) measurably impairs reaction time, memory, and emotional regulation
🚧
Direct Instruction — The Four Sleep Disruptors
10–26 min · Notes · Resource Sheet 6 Part B
▼

Four major lifestyle disruptors — each with a specific biological mechanism. Fill in Resource Sheet 6 Part B as your teacher explains each one.

📱 Screens / Blue Light

480nm blue light hits melanopsin ipRGC cells → signals SCN it's daytime → pineal gland stops melatonin. 30 min at 200 lux delays melatonin onset by ~90 minutes. Content arousal adds a second, separate layer of disruption.

☕ Caffeine

Half-life 5–7 hrs. A 3pm coffee = 50% active at 8–10pm. Blocks adenosine receptors (Process S masking). Also suppresses deep NREM sleep even when you do fall asleep — less glymphatic clearance.

📅 Social Jetlag

Weekend "sleep-ins" shift the circadian clock later — like crossing time zones every Friday. Just 1 hour of social jetlag correlates with 33% higher obesity risk, increased depression, and academic impairment.

🌡 Sleep Environment

Optimal temperature: 18–20°C (core body temp must drop 1–2°C for sleep onset). Even low noise activates cortical arousal without waking. Irregular sleep timing disrupts SCN anticipation → lower sleep efficiency.

Guided Practice — Behaviour Analysis Grid
26–46 min · Individual → groups · Resource Sheet 6 Part C
▼

Analyse these 8 behaviours on your resource sheet. The key rule: you must name the specific molecule or process — not just say "it disrupts sleep".

📱 Using phone in bed until you fall asleep
⚡ Energy drink at 5pm for afternoon sport
🛌 Sleeping in 3 hours on Saturday and Sunday
💤 Napping for 45 min after school (~5pm) — this one is NUANCED
🏃 Exercising intensely at 9pm to de-stress
🌡 Keeping room temperature at 24°C at night
📅 Keeping consistent bed and wake times 7 days a week
🌙 Switching to warm-toned/dim lighting after 8pm
⚠️ The Nap Nuance (Behaviour 4)

A 20-minute nap before 3pm can reduce adenosine and restore alertness. A 45-minute nap at 5pm significantly discharges Process S — making it very hard to fall asleep at a normal bedtime. Timing and duration completely change whether a nap is helpful or harmful.

Critical Thinking — Societal Values & Sleep Research
46–58 min · Wall of Wisdom
▼

Three facts about how society and money shape sleep science. Respond to each:

Fact 1 — The Sleep Economy

The global sleep economy was worth $432 billion in 2019 — apps, supplements, mattresses, wearables. Industry growth depends on people feeling like their sleep is inadequate and fixable by products.

Fact 2 — Popular Science vs Peer Review

Matthew Walker's "Why We Sleep" (2018) became a bestseller, but multiple factual errors went unchallenged in popular media. The gap between what scientists say and what the public hears is often enormous.

Fact 3 — Hustle Culture

"I'll sleep when I'm dead." "Grind season." "5am club." Social media actively glorifies sleep deprivation as a marker of dedication and productivity — directly opposing what sleep science says.
