Key Terms
Prefrontal Cortex
The brain's "CEO" — responsible for impulse control, planning, and emotional regulation. Fully mature around age 25.
Limbic System
The brain's emotional and reward centre — matures earlier than the PFC, creating the adolescent developmental mismatch.
Synaptic Pruning
The brain's "use it or lose it" process — removing unused neural connections to strengthen frequently used ones.
Myelination
The fatty sheath around axons that speeds neural transmission. PFC-limbic myelination is incomplete in adolescence.
Dual Systems Model
A fully accelerated limbic system + underdeveloped PFC = developmental mismatch explaining adolescent risk-taking.
Self-Stigma
When a person internalises society's negative stereotypes about mental illness — "I should be able to handle this."
SEWB Framework
Social and Emotional Wellbeing — the First Nations holistic model integrating connection to country, community, culture, spirit, and body.

Lesson 1 · AC9S10H03 · Explain

The Adolescent Brain —
Under Construction

By the end of this lesson you'll be able to explain the four key developmental processes in the adolescent brain — PFC development, synaptic pruning, limbic hyperactivity, and myelination — and connect each to real adolescent behaviour.

▼

💭 THINK

❓ WONDER
What questions does this raise? What do you want to know more about?

Direct Instruction — Four Key Brain Processes
12–30 min · Notes · Resource Sheet 10
▼

The adolescent brain is not broken or immature — it is in a specific developmental phase with distinct characteristics. Take notes on Resource Sheet 10 as your teacher explains each process.

Prefrontal Cortex (PFC)
Last region to mature (~age 25). Controls impulse regulation, planning, risk assessment, emotional regulation. In adolescence: structurally present but functionally underconnected.
Limbic System
Matures earlier than the PFC. Fully online emotional and reward systems — before the regulatory systems that govern them. Peer presence activates reward circuits more strongly in adolescence than any other age.
Synaptic Pruning
Peak synaptic density ~age 11–12, then aggressive pruning. "Use it or lose it." Makes adolescence a critical sensitive period — habits, skills and thinking patterns reinforced now will persist.
Myelination
Myelin sheaths axons to speed neural transmission. PFC-limbic connection is not fully myelinated in adolescence — the "know better" signal doesn't override the "feels good" signal fast enough.
The Dual Systems Mismatch: A fully accelerated limbic system + an underdeveloped PFC = the adolescent profile. This is not a character flaw. It is a predictable developmental stage.
🔗

30–48 min · Individual · Resource Sheet 10

Taking risks when friends are watching

Discussion — When Does Science Change How We Treat Teenagers?
48–58 min · Class
▼

Three real-world cases where adolescent brain science has — or hasn't — changed policy:

Legal: In 2005, the US Supreme Court ruled that executing people for crimes committed under 18 was unconstitutional — citing brain development research. Neuroscience literally changed law.
Education: Despite strong evidence for later school start times, most Australian secondary schools still begin at 8:30–9:00am. What stops the science from being adopted?
Technology: The neuroscience of adolescent reward sensitivity and social comparison is well established. Legislation on social media age limits still lags significantly behind the evidence.

Adolescent Mental Health —
Risk, Resilience and the Brain

By the end of this lesson you'll be able to analyse the biological, psychological, and social risk and protective factors influencing adolescent mental health — and connect them to the societal values that shape research priorities.

Direct Instruction — Risk and Protective Factors
10–27 min · Notes · Resource Sheet 11
▼

Complete the risk and protective factors grid on Resource Sheet 11 as your teacher explains each. The key framework: biopsychosocial model applied to mental health — no single cause, no single solution.

Biological risk: Genetic predisposition, PFC-limbic imbalance, hormonal changes, chronic sleep deprivation (↑cortisol, ↓PFC regulation)
Psychological risk: Rumination/catastrophising, trauma history, poor emotional regulation, perfectionism and achievement pressure
Social risk: Peer rejection, family conflict, bullying (strongest mental health impact in adolescence), socioeconomic disadvantage
Protective factors: One trusted stable adult (single strongest predictor), sense of belonging, adequate sleep, emotional literacy, cultural identity and connection
🗂
Guided Practice — Case Studies: Claim-Support-Question (PZ Routine)
27–46 min · Individual then pairs · Resource Sheet 11
▼

Your teacher will share four case studies. For each, complete the Claim-Support-Question routine. A Claim is a debatable interpretation. Support is evidence from the case AND from research. Question is what you still don't know.

💬 CLAIM — SUPPORT — QUESTION  |  Project Zero Thinking Routine
💬 CLAIM
I think the most significant risk factor is... (make a specific, debatable interpretation)
📋 SUPPORT
Evidence from the case is... / Research that backs this up is... (distinguish case evidence from research)
❓ QUESTION
A question I still have is... (what don't we know? What would change your claim?)
Case A — Jordan, 15: High-achieving, perfectionist, withdrawing from friends, sleeping 4–5 hours, grades still good, says she "feels nothing"
Case B — Marcus, 16: Parents separated, moved schools, three physical altercations, labelled "disruptive", first-generation immigrant family
Case C — Anika, 17: Openly gay in a conservative community, excellent grades, close friend group, persistent anxiety around family gatherings
Case D — Tyler, 15: Games 8+ hrs daily, limited face-to-face contact, technically gifted, parents work long hours, no family history of mental illness
💰
Critical Thinking — Who Decides What Gets Researched?
46–58 min · Discussion
▼
Fact 1 — Pharmaceutical funding

58% of psychiatry research in top journals (2020) had pharmaceutical industry funding. What types of interventions are likely to be over-represented in the evidence base?

Fact 2 — Cultural representation

Community-based, culturally-specific, and non-Western approaches to mental health are significantly under-researched. Whose values and worldview are embedded in "evidence-based practice"?

Fact 3 — Australian funding gap

Mental health research receives <5% of the total Australian health research budget, despite representing 12% of the burden of disease. What does this tell us about how society values mental health?

✏️
Exit — Analyse Paragraph
58–70 min · Written
▼

Prompt: Analyse the risk and protective factors that influence adolescent mental health. Connect at least ONE biological, ONE psychological, and ONE social factor, and identify a relationship between two of them.

A "relationship" means showing how one factor affects or interacts with another — not just listing them separately. e.g. "Sleep deprivation [biological] increases cortisol which impairs PFC function, making negative cognitive styles [psychological] harder to regulate."

Direct Instruction — Three Layers of Societal Influence
10–26 min · Notes · Resource Sheet 12
▼
Layer 1: Stigma
Public stigma: society's stereotypes
Self-stigma: internalised stereotypes
Structural stigma: discriminatory systems
Stigma is the primary barrier to help-seeking for young people
Layer 2: Systemic Barriers
Cost: ~$100–160 out-of-pocket per session
Wait times: 3–6 months for public services
Geographic: rural/remote access gaps
Cultural safety: mainstream services not universally accessible
Layer 3: Protective Community Factors
Belonging reduces risk independently
Faith communities (when supportive) show protective effects
First Nations SEWB framework: holistic wellbeing model
Community is a health intervention
